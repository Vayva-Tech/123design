# 04A — User Journeys

> Nine documented journeys from entry to conversion. Each defines the visitor's entry point, the question they carry, the content required to answer it, the expected next step, the conversion action, and the failure risks that would cause the visitor to leave without engaging.

---

## Audience Reference

| Code | Audience                              | Profile                                                          |
| ---- | ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A    | Founder / Inventor                    | Has an idea or early concept, needs a partner to make it real    |
| B    | Product / Engineering Director        | Has an engineering org, needs capacity or specialized capability |
| C    | Established Company / Innovation Team | Has resources, needs external development expertise              |
| D    | Operations / Sourcing / Manufacturing | Needs production support, tooling, supplier management           |
| E    | Referral / Returning Client           | Already knows the firm, needs to re-engage                       |

## Journey 1: Founder with Idea

**Audience:** A

**Entry Points:**

- Homepage (direct navigation or referral)
- Google search for "product development firm," "invent a product," "help me make my idea"
- Social media links (Instagram, LinkedIn)

**Core Question:**
"Can these people take my idea and make it real?"

The founder arrives with an abstract concept and no clear understanding of what product development entails. They need to feel welcomed, not intimidated. They need to see that 123.design handles the entire journey from concept to production, and that the firm has done this for people like them before.

**Content Needed:**

- Clear positioning statement — "FROM IDEA TO PRODUCTION" communicates full-journey capability in five words
- Featured work showing concept-to-production range — projects that demonstrate the arc from sketch to finished product
- Process explanation — the CON > EVT > DVT > PVT > Production framework demystifies what happens after "I have an idea"
- Approachable tone throughout — this visitor is not an engineer; language must be accessible without being condescending
- Start Project funnel as a visible, approachable entry point — someone who doesn't know where to begin needs a clear door

**Next Step:**
Explore work > Read process > Start Project

The founder's ideal path is: see proof it can be done > understand how it works > take the first step. Each content block must lead naturally to the next.

**Conversion:**
START A PROJECT funnel submission

**Failure Risks:**

- **Too corporate or intimidating** — If the homepage feels like an enterprise consulting firm, the founder will self-select out. The tone must be confident but approachable.
- **No clear entry point** — If the founder cannot find "Start Project" within two scrolls, the page has failed. The CTA must be visible and inviting.
- **Jargon-heavy language** — Terms like "DFM," "RTV tooling," or "PVT validation" mean nothing to this visitor. Language must be plain and outcome-oriented.
- **No visible path from idea to product** — If the site doesn't clearly show that 123.design handles the full journey, the founder will assume they need multiple firms and leave.
- **No relevant work** — If nothing in the portfolio resembles a consumer product or early-stage invention, the founder has no evidence this firm works with people like them.

## Journey 2: Engineering Director Needing Capacity

**Audience:** B

**Entry Points:**

- Capability page (specific discipline: mechanical engineering, electrical engineering, etc.)
- Google search on specific capability ("mechanical engineering firm," "product development partner")
- Referral from a colleague or industry contact

**Core Question:**
"Can this firm augment my team with real engineering depth?"

This visitor is technical. They are evaluating engineering competence, not marketing polish. They need to assess whether 123.design can integrate with their existing team, work within their processes, and deliver at the level of rigor their organization requires.

**Content Needed:**

- Technical capability detail — each capability page must go beyond category names into actual engineering substance: methods, tools, outputs, constraints
- Lifecycle stages — CON > EVT > DVT > PVT > Production demonstrates structured methodology that maps to how engineering directors think about development
- "Your process or ours" messaging — critical for this visitor; they need to know 123.design can integrate with their Jira, their review cycles, their documentation standards, not just operate independently
- Related project evidence — case studies showing technical depth, engineering decisions, trade-off reasoning, not just polished renders
- Engineering specificity — mention of real tools (CAD, FEA, CFD), real techniques, real tolerances

**Next Step:**
Read capability > View related projects > Start Project or Schedule Conversation

This visitor may want a conversation before committing to a form. Both paths must be available.

**Conversion:**
START A PROJECT or Schedule Conversation

**Failure Risks:**

- **Too superficial** — Capability pages that read like marketing copy signal shallow thinking. Engineering directors read technical content; vague language triggers immediate departure.
- **No technical depth** — If the mechanical engineering page doesn't mention specific techniques, tools, or engineering decisions, it's not credible to this visitor.
- **No project evidence** — Claims without case study backup are empty. This visitor needs to see what the firm has actually built, not what it says it can do.
- **Vague claims** — "We do great engineering" without specificity is meaningless. Every claim needs evidence or technical grounding.
- **No integration story** — If the site doesn't address how 123.design works with existing teams and workflows, the engineering director will assume a difficult engagement and look elsewhere.

## Journey 3: Company Seeking Prototyping

**Audience:** B / C

**Entry Points:**

- Prototyping capability page (`/capabilities/prototyping`)
- Google search ("rapid prototyping," "prototype manufacturer," "FDM prototyping services")
- Process page (discovered prototyping as part of the development lifecycle)

**Core Question:**
"Can they prototype what I need quickly and well?"

This visitor has a specific need: physical prototypes, produced fast, at quality. They want to know what techniques are available, what the output quality looks like, and whether the firm has done something similar before.

**Content Needed:**

- Prototyping techniques with specificity:
  - **FDM** — fused deposition modeling, thermoplastic materials, functional prototypes
  - **SLA / SLS** — stereolithography and selective laser sintering, high-resolution surfaces, complex geometries
  - **RTV Tooling** — room-temperature vulcanization silicone molds for low-volume urethane casting
  - **Sheet Metal** — laser cutting, bending, welding for enclosures and brackets
  - **Composites** — layup techniques, fiber reinforcement, structural prototypes
- Process gallery — real images of real prototypes from the 123.design shop floor, not stock photography. The 18 verified prototyping assets from the Phase 0B.3 audit are the source.
- Speed and quality evidence — lead time ranges, finish quality, tolerance capabilities
- Related project examples — case studies where prototyping was a key deliverable or differentiator

**Next Step:**
View prototyping capability > See process techniques and gallery > Start Project

**Conversion:**
START A PROJECT

**Failure Risks:**

- **No technique detail** — Listing "FDM" without explaining what it produces, what materials, what use cases is meaningless to a technical buyer. Each technique needs context.
- **No real process images** — Stock photography of 3D printers is not evidence. The gallery must show actual work from the 123.design shop.
- **Generic "we prototype things" language** — This visitor is comparing firms. A thin prototyping page signals that prototyping is an afterthought, not a core capability.
- **No quality evidence** — If the visitor can't assess surface finish, tolerance, or material quality from the page, they have no basis for comparison.
- **Page feels thin** — A capability page with one paragraph and a gallery is not credible. The prototyping page needs depth: techniques, materials, quality, lead times, and project evidence.

## Journey 4: Company Seeking Manufacturing Support

**Audience:** D

**Entry Points:**

- Manufacturing capability page (`/capabilities/manufacturing`)
- Industry page (discovered manufacturing within an industry context)
- Google search ("contract manufacturing," "DFM services," "product manufacturing partner")

**Core Question:**
"Can they manage production for us?"

This visitor needs a partner who can take a design into production: handle DFM analysis, coordinate tooling, manage supplier relationships, oversee pilot builds, and ensure quality at volume. They need evidence that 123.design has done this successfully.

**Content Needed:**

- Manufacturing capability detail:
  - **DFM (Design for Manufacturing)** — how design decisions affect manufacturability, cost, and quality
  - **Tooling** — mold design, tool fabrication, tool management
  - **Supplier coordination** — sourcing, qualification, production oversight
  - **Pilot builds** — bridging prototype to production
  - **Quality** — inspection, testing, process control at volume
- Related project evidence with manufacturing outcomes — not just "we designed it" but "we brought it to production." The visitor needs to see production results, not just prototypes.
- Production specificity — materials, processes, volumes, geographies where the firm has actual experience

**Next Step:**
Read manufacturing capability > View related projects with production outcomes > Start Project

**Conversion:**
START A PROJECT

**Failure Risks:**

- **No manufacturing evidence** — Claims of manufacturing capability without project proof are empty. This visitor is operations-focused; they need to see production results.
- **Overclaiming factory relationships** — "We work with factories worldwide" without specificity signals hand-waving. The visitor needs to know where, what kind, what volumes.
- **No production examples** — If every project in the portfolio stops at prototype stage, this visitor has no evidence the firm can deliver production.
- **No DFM explanation** — If the page doesn't explain how design decisions affect manufacturability, cost, and quality, it's not credible to an operations professional.
- **Unverified claims** — Production volumes, factory geography, or certification claims without owner verification are a liability.

## Journey 5: Medical Product Team

**Audience:** C

**Entry Points:**

- Medical industry page (`/industries/medical`)
- Google search ("medical device development," "product development for medical devices")
- Referral from industry contact

**Core Question:**
"Do they understand medical device development constraints?"

This visitor is trained to spot overclaiming. They work in a regulated environment where precision, documentation, and validation matter. They need to see that 123.design understands the constraints of medical product development without claiming certifications or regulatory expertise the firm does not hold.

**Content Needed:**

- Medical industry page with careful, measured language — every word is scrutinized by this visitor. Precision and restraint signal competence.
- Relevant engineering capabilities:
  - Product Development — structured lifecycle approach
  - Mechanical Engineering — enclosure design, mechanism design, materials selection
  - Electrical Engineering — PCB design, firmware, sensor integration
  - Testing & Validation — DVT and PVT stages are particularly relevant; the validation framework signals regulatory awareness
  - Manufacturing — production realization for regulated products
- Process showing validation stages — CON > EVT > DVT > PVT > Production; the DVT and PVT stages map directly to the validation thinking this visitor expects
- **Careful language about regulatory awareness** — The firm can demonstrate understanding of the development constraints (documentation, traceability, validation) without claiming FDA, UL, or ISO certification
- **No unverified certification claims** — Never claim regulatory certifications without explicit owner verification

**Next Step:**
Read medical industry page > Understand relevant capabilities > Schedule Conversation

**Conversion:**
Schedule Conversation (lower commitment than START A PROJECT)

This visitor needs a conversation before committing to a project form. The medical context requires relationship-building and mutual assessment before formal engagement.

**Failure Risks:**

- **Overclaiming regulatory expertise** — Claiming FDA, UL, or ISO certification without explicit owner verification is disqualifying. This visitor will verify claims and leave immediately if they're false.
- **No careful language** — Casual or imprecise language signals that the firm doesn't understand the rigor required in medical development.
- **Projects published without approval** — Medical work may be under NDA or require client permission. Publishing without approval is a legal and ethical risk.
- **No validation/testing depth** — If the Testing & Validation capability page is thin, the medical visitor has no reason to believe the firm can support the validation burden.
- **Language too sales-oriented** — This visitor expects a peer-level technical conversation, not a marketing pitch.

## Journey 6: Defense / Security Team

**Audience:** C

**Entry Points:**

- Defense industry page (`/industries/defense-security`)
- Google search ("defense product development," "secure engineering firm")
- Referral from industry contact or cleared colleague

**Core Question:**
"Can they work within security and compliance constraints?"

This visitor operates in an environment where confidentiality, discretion, and process rigor are non-negotiable. They need to know that 123.design understands the constraints of defense and security product development without disclosing sensitive information or overclaiming clearances and relationships.

**Content Needed:**

- Defense industry page with careful confidentiality language — this visitor needs to know the firm understands discretion as a core operating principle
- Relevant capabilities:
  - Structured development process — CON > EVT > DVT > PVT > Production signals rigor and traceability
  - Testing & Validation — defense products require extensive validation
  - Manufacturing — production realization within controlled supply chains
  - Electrical / Mechanical Engineering — core technical capability
- Technical capability without sensitive disclosure — the page must demonstrate competence without revealing details of past classified or sensitive work
- **No disclosure of sensitive information** — Defense projects are often classified; any project reference must be owner-approved and carefully scoped
- **No overclaiming clearance or relationships** — Never claim security clearances, government relationships, or certifications without explicit owner verification

**Next Step:**
Read defense industry page > Understand capabilities > Schedule Conversation

**Conversion:**
Schedule Conversation (lower commitment)

This visitor will not submit a detailed project form about a classified or sensitive project. The conversation-first approach is essential.

**Failure Risks:**

- **Disclosing sensitive information** — Any reference to specific defense projects, clients, or capabilities without owner approval is a serious breach.
- **Overclaiming clearance or relationships** — Claiming security clearances, facility accreditations, or government partnerships without verification is disqualifying.
- **Projects published without approval** — Defense work requires explicit client permission and careful scope review before any public disclosure.
- **Language too casual** — Defense visitors expect formality, precision, and discretion in every word.
- **No evidence of structured process** — Without process depth, the firm appears ad-hoc and unreliable. Defense development requires documentation, traceability, and rigor.

## Journey 7: Visitor Arriving on Case Study from Google

**Audience:** A / B

**Entry Points:**

- Project detail page (`/work/[project-slug]`) via Google search
- Social media share of a specific project
- Referral link to a specific case study

**Core Question:**
"Is this relevant to my situation? Can they do something similar for me?"

This visitor did not start at the homepage. They arrived at a specific project because Google surfaced it for their search. They need to immediately understand: what is this project, is it relevant to me, how deep did the firm go, and can they do something similar for me?

**Content Needed:**

- Strong project hero with media — first impression must communicate real, physical, engineered work. The hero image or video is the primary engagement hook.
- Clear project summary — verified overview that explains the project scope, challenge, and outcome in plain language
- Key facts — industry, services provided, timeline, materials, or other distinguishing details
- Optional depth modules (only if populated): challenge, engineering approach, prototyping process, manufacturing outcome, results. Empty modules are worse than absent modules.
- Related capabilities — the visitor needs a path from "this project" to "what else can they do for me"
- Related projects — keep the visitor moving through the work archive; if this project isn't relevant, another might be
- Clear next step — START A PROJECT CTA at the end of the case study, or a prompt to explore more work

**Next Step:**
Read case study > Explore related capabilities > View more work > Start Project

**Conversion:**
START A PROJECT (at case study ending)

**Failure Risks:**

- **Weak project hero** — If the hero image is low-quality, generic, or missing, the visitor bounces immediately. The hero is the single most important element on the page.
- **No clear next step** — If the case study doesn't lead to START A PROJECT or another project, the visitor leaves the site entirely.
- **No related content** — A case study without related capabilities or projects is a dead end. The visitor needs multiple paths forward.
- **Metadata wall before imagery on mobile** — If the mobile experience shows project metadata (industry, services, dates) before the hero image, the visual impact is destroyed. Imagery first, details after.
- **Project feels fabricated** — Unverified details, stock imagery, or vague language destroys credibility instantly. This visitor arrived from Google; they are evaluating authenticity.
- **Empty modules visible** — A "Manufacturing" module that says "coming soon" or has placeholder text is worse than no module at all. Only render populated modules.

## Journey 8: Visitor Arriving on Capability Article

**Audience:** B / C

**Entry Points:**

- Capability detail page (`/capabilities/[capability-slug]`) via Google
- Insights article (`/insights/[article-slug]`) via Google or social media
- Search for specific technical topic ("injection molding design guide," "DFM best practices")

**Core Question:**
"Do they actually know this domain?"

This visitor arrived because the page title or search snippet suggested expertise. They need the content to deliver substance, not surface-level overview. They are evaluating whether the firm's claimed capabilities are backed by real knowledge.

**Content Needed:**

- Technical depth — the content must demonstrate actual domain knowledge. If the capability page or article could have been written by anyone, it proves nothing.
- Related project evidence — capability claims need project backup. "We do injection molding design" is a claim; "Here's a project where our DFM analysis reduced cycle time by 30%" is evidence.
- Clear capability scope — what the firm does, what it doesn't, what the engagement looks like. This visitor needs to assess fit.
- Related capabilities — cross-links to adjacent disciplines show the integrated model
- CTA — a path from "this is credible" to "let's talk about my project"

**Next Step:**
Read capability or article > View related projects > Start Project

**Conversion:**
START A PROJECT

**Failure Risks:**

- **Shallow content** — A capability page with one paragraph and a list of services is not credible. This visitor is comparing firms; thin content signals thin capability.
- **No project evidence** — Capability claims without project backup are marketing, not substance. The visitor needs to see what the firm has actually built.
- **Jargon without substance** — Technical terms without context or explanation signal that the firm is performing expertise, not demonstrating it.
- **No clear path to conversion** — If the visitor finishes the article or capability page and has no clear next step, they leave. Every content page needs a CTA.
- **No author credibility** — Anonymous technical content carries less weight than attributed expertise. If the author is verified and named, attribution adds trust.

## Journey 9: Returning Client Seeking Contact

**Audience:** E

**Entry Points:**

- Contact page (`/contact`)
- Footer link (on any page)
- Direct navigation (bookmarked URL or typed directly)
- Google search for "123.design contact"

**Core Question:**
"How do I reach them?"

This visitor already knows the firm. They may be a returning client with a new project, a referral contact, or someone who worked with 123.design before and needs to reconnect. They have a simple need: find verified contact information quickly.

**Content Needed:**

- Verified contact data only — email, phone, address. Only information the owner has explicitly approved for publication.
- Clear, prominent presentation — contact information should be findable within seconds, not buried in page content.
- Start Project prompt — if the returning visitor has a new project, the path to START A PROJECT should be available alongside contact information.
- Existing client acknowledgment — the page should feel like it recognizes returning visitors, not treat them as strangers. A returning client should not have to re-qualify through the full funnel.

**Next Step:**
Find contact info > Reach out directly, or Start a Project for new work

**Conversion:**
Contact (email, phone) or START A PROJECT if new project

**Failure Risks:**

- **Contact info not visible** — If the visitor cannot find a phone number or email within seconds, the page has failed its primary purpose.
- **Speculative office locations** — Never publish an address or phone number without owner verification. Incorrect contact information is worse than no contact information.
- **No verified data** — If no contact data has been verified by the owner, the page should show only what is confirmed and prompt the visitor to use the Start Project form.
- **Start Project funnel too complex for existing client** — A returning client should be able to start a new project without re-answering every qualification question. The funnel should recognize returning visitors where possible.
- **No path to human contact** — If the contact page is all form and no human information, the visitor feels like they're submitting into a void.

## Cross-Journey Patterns

### Conversion Hierarchy

Not all journeys convert the same way:

1. **START A PROJECT** — Primary conversion for audiences A, B, D, and journeys 1-4, 7-8. The visitor is ready to engage.
2. **Schedule Conversation** — Secondary conversion for audiences C in regulated industries (medical, defense) and journeys 5-6. The visitor needs relationship-building before formal engagement.
3. **Contact** — Tertiary conversion for audience E and journey 9. The visitor already knows the firm and needs a direct line.

### Content Dependencies

Every journey depends on:

- **Published projects** — Without real project evidence, no journey can succeed. Projects are the proof that validates every claim.
- **Capability depth** — Capability pages must go beyond category names into technical substance.
- **Process clarity** — The CON > EVT > DVT > PVT > Production framework is a differentiator across all journeys.
- **Verified contact data** — Every journey eventually needs a path to human contact.

### Universal Failure Conditions

Regardless of journey, these conditions cause departure:

- No visible path forward (dead ends)
- Claims without evidence
- Unverified or fabricated content
- Broken or missing navigation
- Slow or broken page loads
- Content that doesn't match the entry promise (e.g., Google snippet says "medical device expertise" but the page is generic)
